   \abstract
   {Non-grey radiative transfer in radiation-hydrodynamics (RHD) and
    magneto-hydrodynamics (MHD) simulations of stellar atmospheres is
    usually treated by opacity binning: the opacity distribution function
    is sorted into a small number of $(\tau,\lambda)$ groups, each
    represented by a Planck- or Rosseland-mean opacity \citep{nordlund1982,ludwig1992,vogler2004}.
    The placement of the bin edges is the central degree of freedom of the
    method and is conventionally chosen by hand using physical heuristics.}
   {We aim to find the partition of the
    $(\tau,\lambda)$ plane that minimizes the error in the
    simulated radiative heating rate $Q_\mathrm{rad}$ with respect to the
    full line-by-line reference, and to learn which ingredients of the
    search actually matter: which stages find the good tilings, and which
    variants fail.}
   {We recast opacity binning as a partition of the
    $(-\log_{10}\tau,\,\log_{10}\lambda)$ plane into rectangular groups,
    the leaves of a guillotine tree, and optimize that partition directly
    against the $Q_\mathrm{rad}$ residual from a 1D
    short-characteristics radiative-transfer solve per candidate binning.
    The pipeline is staged seeding (3 $\tau$ cuts, a 64-sample
    Latin-hypercube $\tau$ scan, $\tau$ polish, then a flipped
    bottom-region fine-grid scan with the top cut frozen), followed by
    beam-3 grow, position polish, and a topology search over
    SPLIT, TRANSPLANT and REALLOC moves. The leaf cap is 5 groups at a
    budget of 800 evaluations. The setup is the solar-like model
    G2 on a $300\times150$ $(T,p)$ ODF grid with 328 wavelength bins of
    12 sub-bins each, scored by the rms $Q_\mathrm{rad}$ residual times an
    empty-band penalty.}
   {Across 25 seeds the early checkpoints tie ($\tau$ scan near
    $1.318\times10^{8}$, staged $\tau$ near $1.242\times10^{8}$), the
    staged-$\lambda$ checkpoint fans out from $1.01$ to
    $1.48\times10^{8}$, and the finals scatter from $7.9\times10^{7}$ to
    $1.2\times10^{8}$. One seed reached $4.87\times10^{7}$, about 63\%
    below its seed rms, through a REALLOC cliff late in the run; a
    re-run of seeds 0 to 4 on the final code gave finals of 4.92, 4.37,
    4.71, 4.71 and $4.71\times10^{7}$, with $4.37\times10^{7}$ the best
    residual recorded. An ablation on the winner shows the within-group
    low/mid/high opacity split carries roughly four-fifths of the
    accuracy: disabling it degrades $4.87\times10^{7}$ to
    $2.42\times10^{8}$, and collapsing a single group already costs a
   third. Staging selects the basin and downstream search
   cannot leave it; early-selection portfolios, column-constrained
   tilings and grid search all failed to reach the optimum and were
   removed. Repeating the cap ladder on the K dwarf K4 finds its own
   asymmetric optimum ($3.46\times10^{7}$ at cap 5), but neither star's
   optimum transfers (foreign losses of 3 to 5); only flatter mid-tier
   tilings score near $10^{8}$ on both atmospheres.}
   {Minimizing the transfer residual directly, with staged seeding plus
   beam grow plus topology REALLOC, finds tilings far better than
   hand-placed edges at the same band count. The open problem is basin
   selection: nothing cheap predicts which staging will refine well, so
   future work must address late selection, wider transplant reach,
   basin diversity in the beam, objective shaping, or joint optimization
   over several atmospheres at once.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

   Time-dependent, multi-dimensional simulations of stellar surface
   convection require a treatment of radiative transfer that is both
   non-grey and affordable. The dominant approach is \emph{opacity
   binning} \citep{nordlund1982,ludwig1992,ludwig1994,skartlien2000,vogler2004}: the
   monochromatic opacity distribution function (ODF) is sorted into a
   modest number of groups, and each group is represented by a single
   mean opacity (a Planck mean where the gas is optically thin and a
   Rosseland mean where it is optically thick, blended smoothly between
   the two regimes \citep[Eq.~12]{vogler2004}. The resulting handful of
   opacities then replaces the full spectrum in the radiative-transfer
   module of the simulation code \citep[e.g.][]{vogler2005,magic2013}.

   Within this framework the central choice is how to \emph{partition}
   the ODF into groups: which wavelength intervals, sorted by the optical
   depth they reach in a reference atmosphere, are merged together.
   Conventional practice fixes a set of $\tau$-group edges by hand,
   informed by physical intuition about where the photosphere forms, and
   optionally subdivides some groups in wavelength \citep{vogler2004}.
   The quality of a binning is judged by how well the binned calculation
   reproduces the radiative heating rate $Q_\mathrm{rad}$ of the full,
   line-by-line calculation, but the edges themselves are rarely chosen
   by optimizing that quantity directly.

   Here we take the partition to be an optimization variable. We
   represent the binning as a guillotine tree that tiles the
   $(-\log_{10}\tau,\,\log_{10}\lambda)$ plane into rectangular
   $(\tau,\lambda)$ groups, and we drive a staged non-greedy search over
   tree topologies and cut positions with the actual $Q_\mathrm{rad}$
   residual computed from a radiative-transfer solve for every candidate
   binning. The question we address is practical: for a given number of
   bands, which $(\tau,\lambda)$ tiling best reproduces the full-ODF
   heating rate, which stages of the search find it, and which plausible
   shortcuts fail. The campaign we report used a leaf cap of 5 groups
   and a budget of 800 evaluations per run, repeated over 25 seeds.

   Section~\ref{sec:method} reviews opacity binning and introduces the
   tree partition, the objective, and the search algorithm.
   Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the validation setup.
   Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results, including the best
   tiling found. Section~\ref{sec:negative} reports the variants that
   failed. Section~\ref{sec:cross} repeats the ladder on a K dwarf model
   and scores each atmosphere's winners on the other, which we discuss in
   Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion}. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Opacity binning}
\label{sec:binning}

   We adopt the formulation of \citet{vogler2004} (Eqs.~6, 11, 12, 16
   and 17 in that paper). The ODF supplies, on a
   grid of temperatures and pressures, a line opacity for each of
   $N_\mathrm{bin}$ wavelength intervals (\emph{bins}), each subdivided
   into $N_\mathrm{sub}$ \emph{sub-bins} carrying a fractional width. The
   continuum opacity is added bin-by-bin. For each ODF step (a sub-bin at
   a given $T,p$) we evaluate the Rosseland optical depth
   $\tau=\kappa_\mathrm{Ross}\,\rho\,H$ it reaches in a reference
   atmosphere, where $H$ is the local pressure scale height, and sort the
   steps into $\tau$-groups.

   For a group $l$, the band-integrated Planck source and the Planck-mean
   opacity are \citep[Eqs.~16 and 17]{vogler2004}
   \begin{align}
      B_l      &= \sum_i \Delta\nu_i\, B_{\Delta\nu_i}
                  \sum_{j(i,l)} w_{j(i,l)}, \label{eq:Bl}\\
      \bar\kappa_{P,l}
               &= \frac{1}{B_l}\sum_i \Delta\nu_i\, B_{\Delta\nu_i}
                  \sum_{j(i,l)} w_{j(i,l)}\,\kappa_{ij(i,l)},
                  \label{eq:kappap}
   \end{align}
   where the inner sum runs over the ODF steps $j(i,l)$ that fall into
   group $l$ within wavelength bin $i$, $w$ is the sub-bin weight, and
   $B_{\Delta\nu_i}$ is the Planck function averaged over the bin. The
   Rosseland-mean opacity is defined analogously with the Planck
   derivative $dB/dT$ as the weight.

   The two means are blended as a function of the optical depth a group
   reaches \citep[Eq.~12]{vogler2004}:
   \begin{equation}
      \bar\kappa_i = 2^{-\tau_i/\tau_0}\,\bar\kappa_{P,i}
                   + \bigl(1 - 2^{-\tau_i/\tau_0}\bigr)\,\bar\kappa_{R,i},
      \label{eq:blend}
   \end{equation}
   with the transition scale $\tau_0 = 0.35$ \citep{ludwig1992,vogler2004}.
   The group mean $\bar\kappa_i$ is what enters the multi-group
   radiative-transfer solve.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The binning as a guillotine-tree partition}
\label{sec:tree}

   The assignment of ODF steps to groups is entirely determined by the
   partition of the $(-\log_{10}\tau,\,\log_{10}\lambda)$ plane into
   rectangles. We represent any such partition as a \emph{guillotine
   tree}: starting from the bounding window, each interior node applies a
   single cut, either along $\tau$ (a horizontal line) or along
   $\lambda$ (a vertical line), recursing on the two children until the
   leaves are the $(\tau,\lambda)$ groups (Fig.~\ref{fig:binning}).
   representation can express any rectangular tiling, including, as
   special, restricted cases, the layouts used in earlier work: a single
   $\tau$ partition (one $\lambda$ cell), a shared $\tau$ partition with a
   per-group wavelength-split flag, and a per-$\tau$-group wavelength
   edge. We adopt the guillotine tree as the single internal
   representation and number its leaves in $\tau$-major depth-first order.

   Within each group, the sub-bins are further segmented along the
   sorted-opacity axis into three opacity segments (low, mid, high),
   so that the final band count is $N_\mathrm{bands} =
   N_\mathrm{splits}\,N_\mathrm{groups}$ with
   $N_\mathrm{splits}=3$; a group whose bottom-of-atmosphere
   weighted-opacity dynamic range falls below the
   min-opacity-delta threshold is left unsplit and keeps a single band
   (Fig.~\ref{fig:sorted}). The split is the dominant accuracy lever in
   our setup: disabling it on the winning tiling degrades the rms from
   $4.87\times10^{7}$ to $2.42\times10^{8}$ (Sect.~\ref{sec:split}), so
   roughly four-fifths of the winner's accuracy comes from the opacity
   split and one-fifth from the $(\tau,\lambda)$ tiling.

\subsection{Objective: the radiative heating-rate residual}
\label{sec:objective}

   We judge a binning by the quantity of interest: how well the binned
   opacities reproduce the radiative heating rate of the full ODF. For a
   given binning we build the group-mean opacities
   (Eqs.~\ref{eq:Bl} and \ref{eq:blend}), interpolate them onto the
   atmospheric grid, solve the 1D radiative-transfer equation per band by
   short characteristics \citep{kunasz1988,bruls1999}, and sum the
   per-band heating rate into $Q_\mathrm{rad}^{\mathrm{bin}}$. The
   radiative heating rate per unit mass \citep{mihalas1978} is
   \begin{equation}
      \frac{Q_\mathrm{rad}}{\rho}
      = \frac{4\pi}{\rho}\int \kappa_\nu\bigl(J_\nu - S_\nu\bigr)\,d\nu,
      \label{eq:qrad}
   \end{equation}
   evaluated in the binned form with the band-mean opacities and
   band-integrated source functions. The objective is the root-mean-square
   residual against the full-ODF reference $Q_\mathrm{rad}^{\mathrm{full}}$,
   \begin{equation}
      \mathcal{R}(\text{binning})
      = \mathrm{rms}_{\,\log_{10}\tau_\mathrm{Ross}\in W}
        \!\left[\bigl(Q_\mathrm{rad}^{\mathrm{bin}}
        - Q_\mathrm{rad}^{\mathrm{full}}\bigr)/\rho\right],
      \label{eq:objective}
   \end{equation}
   over the window $W=[-5,\,3]$ of the Rosseland optical-depth axis.
   Alternative
   metrics (maximum absolute residual, integral of $|Q|$) are available
   but we use the rms throughout. The score the search minimizes
   multiplies this rms by $(1 + 0.75\,n_\mathrm{empty})$, where
   $n_\mathrm{empty}$ counts bands containing no ODF steps, so the search
   cannot collapse groups into empty bands for a spurious gain.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Search: staged seeding, grow, polish, topology}
\label{sec:search}

   We minimize the penalized $\mathcal{R}$ over guillotine trees in a
   staged pipeline. \emph{Seed}: the tree starts from 3 equally spaced
   $\tau$ cuts over the outer window (full $\lambda$ width, no
   $\lambda$ cuts). A Latin-hypercube scan of 64 $\tau$-cut sets
   replaces the uniform seed with its winner. \emph{$\tau$ polish}: the
   $\tau$-only tree is position polished. \emph{Flipped bottom-region
   scan}: the top (root $\tau$) cut is frozen, and a deterministic
   fine-grid scan over the photospheric region enumerates
   wavelength-cut and per-column $\tau$-cut combinations with asymmetric
   allocations (the $\tau$ depth stacked inside one $\lambda$ column,
   the other column left undivided). The winner becomes the staged
   seed, and the frozen top cut is never moved or removed again (a
   --no-freeze-top variant that leaves it movable ties on two seeds but
   stalls at $8.61\times10^{7}$ against $4.75\times10^{7}$ on a third,
   so freezing stays the default).
   \emph{Grow}: the tree is expanded by splitting leaves, using a
   non-greedy \emph{beam search}: up to 3 rival topologies are
   kept in parallel, several split positions are tried per candidate leaf
   and axis, and topologies are de-duplicated by their leaf-rectangle
   signature. The greedy limit (beam width 1) splits the widest leaf at
   its midpoint and commits a split only if $\mathcal{R}$ improves. Each
   candidate split is one full evaluation of Eq.~\ref{eq:objective}.
   \emph{Topology}: a structural pass tries SPLIT (cut a leaf on either
   axis under the cap), TRANSPLANT (move $\tau$ depth across a shared
   $\lambda$ cut: collapse a $\tau$ cut inside one column and re-cut the
   other, with the shared $\lambda$ cut jointly polished), and REALLOC
   (drop a collapsible cut and re-cut a leaf, typically on the other
   axis, for a different topology under the same leaf budget). Each
   candidate move is polished to its position optimum before scoring.

   Two guardrails keep the search well-posed: cut positions must remain
   strictly increasing and separated by a per-axis minimum gap, and the
   empty-band penalty of Sect.~\ref{sec:objective} stops the search from
   collapsing groups. The leaf count is capped at 5 groups. Each
   evaluation costs a complete radiative-transfer solve (about 1.5 s
   here), so the search runs as a bounded batch job of at most 800
   evaluations. Progress is recorded at the checkpoints start,
   tau-scan, staged-tau, staged-lambda, grow, blocks and topo
   (Appendix~\ref{app:search}).

\section{Validation setup}
\label{sec:setup}

   The binning and the radiative transfer both run on a single 1D
   plane-parallel model atmosphere, the solar-like model G2 (columns
   height, density, pressure, temperature, top of atmosphere first). The
   ODF is the $300\times150$ $(T,p)$ grid over $328$ wavelength bins of
   $12$ sub-bins each; the continuum absorption is added bin-by-bin. The
   full-ODF reference $Q_\mathrm{rad}^{\mathrm{full}}$ is computed by the
   same transfer solver with the ungrouped opacities, and a grey
   (single Rosseland-mean) calculation provides a one-band baseline.

   The campaign ran the staged pipeline of Sect.~\ref{sec:search} at leaf
   cap 5 and budget 800 over 25 seeds (seeds 0 to 4 plus sweep seeds 5 to
   24), logging the rms at every checkpoint. After dead code paths were
   cut from the optimizer (Sect.~\ref{sec:negative}), seeds 0 to 4 were
   re-run on the final code for verification. A separate run at leaf cap
   8 tested whether the group count binds.

   The checkpoints tell a consistent story across the 25 seeds

   ties near $1.242\times10^{8}$. The staged-$\lambda$ scan is where the

   polish. A control re-run from the seed-3 staging reproduces
   $4.87\times10^{7}$ exactly.

   the shift. Seed 1 at $4.37\times10^{7}$ is the best rms recorded on

\subsection{How much of the win is the opacity split?}
\label{sec:split}

   The results above entangle two choices: the $(\tau,\lambda)$ tiling
   and the within-group low/mid/high opacity split. Re-scoring the fixed
   winning tiling with the split progressively disabled separates them.
   Collapsing one group (the smallest dynamic range, $7.9\times10^{2}$)
   to a single band costs a third ($4.87\times10^{7}$ to
   $6.50\times10^{7}$); collapsing two costs a factor of two
   ($9.88\times10^{7}$); collapsing three or more saturates near
   $2.3\times10^{8}$, and disabling the split everywhere gives
   $2.42\times10^{8}$, a factor of $5.0$ above the winner
   (Fig.~\ref{fig:split}). The per-group dynamic ranges span
   $7.9\times10^{2}$ to $1.3\times10^{8}$, so the default threshold
   splits every group and coincides with a threshold of $20$ on this
   tiling. The tiling search matters, but the split does most of the
   work.

   The split boundaries come from a least-squares piecewise-linear fit
   to the log-sorted opacity curve after a moving-average smoothing;
   fixed-fraction alternatives (equal-count, equal-log-width, two or
   four segments) all score $8.9\times10^{7}$ to $2.5\times10^{8}$ on
   the winner, and the same fitted machinery on a raw-$\kappa$ rank
   scores $8.9\times10^{7}$ against $4.87\times10^{7}$, so the power
   lies in the fit to the Planck-weighted rank, not the segment count.
   Widening the smoothing window from 7 to 15 members gains a further
   4.7\% on the winner ($4.87\times10^{7}$ to $4.64\times10^{7}$) by
   moving only the high-tail cut of one group; re-scoring all five seed
   tilings at both windows never regresses by more than 0.2\%, and
   scoring every full-search final under both windows favors 15 on five
   of six tilings, including the campaign best
   ($4.51\times10^{7}$). Full searches at both settings confirm the gain
   survives the search: window 15 escapes a dead basin the default
   stalls in ($8.44\times10^{7}$ to $5.05\times10^{7}$ on one seed). The
   one deficit is search luck, a missed basin the scorer itself rates
   better, so 15 is now the default.

\section{Negative results}
\label{sec:negative}

   Three findings shaped the final pipeline by killing plausible
   alternatives. First, staging selects the basin and downstream search
   cannot leave it. Grow, polish and topology only refine within-basin: a
   seed-0 control run moved from $1.48\times10^{8}$ to $1.02\times10^{8}$
   and stalled at the pre-transplant level, while the seed-3 control
   reproduces $4.87\times10^{7}$ exactly. The missing move is a
   cross-column depth reallocation (remove a $\tau$ cut from inside one
   $\lambda$ column, insert it into the other, jointly polish the shared
   cut). We implemented it as TRANSPLANT, with a regression test on an
   analytic bowl, but the seed-0 control shows it does not rescue far
   stagings on real data: its reach is one collapse plus recut plus a
   short joint walk.

   Second, a staged portfolio with early selection picks the wrong
   winner. Running staging five times and keeping the best
   staged-$\lambda$ checkpoint put all five portfolio runs at
   $1.01$ to $1.13\times10^{8}$, worse than the singles, and seed 3 lost
   its basin ($1.10\times10^{8}$ against $4.87\times10^{7}$ for the
   single). The logs show why: the portfolio reproduced the winning
   staging (member 0 at $1.1069\times10^{8}$) but selected member 1
   ($1.1014\times10^{8}$, marginally better raw) whose basin is dead.
   Raw staged-$\lambda$ rms anti-predicts the post-topology outcome
   ($1.1014\times10^{8} \to 1.10\times10^{8}$ versus $1.1069\times10^{8}
   \to 4.87\times10^{7}$). Selection fires before the property that
   matters is visible. Late selection, refining every member through
   topology, would cost about five full budgets. The portfolio option
   and its tests were deleted.

   Third, the group count is not the binding constraint; the allocation
   is. Raising the leaf cap from 3 to 4 moves the floor only from about
   $1.2\times10^{8}$ to $1.0\times10^{8}$, while cap 5 reaches
   $4.4$--$5.1\times10^{7}$: the jump comes from landing the asymmetric
   allocation, not from adding leaves (Fig.~\ref{fig:caps}). What distinguishes
   the winning basin is where the depth resolution sits (stacked where
   the sub-bin density lives, sparse regions whole), and no cheap proxy
   for that transplantability exists: the optimizer cannot rank basins
   without refining them.

   \begin{figure}[t]
      \includegraphics[width=\hsize]{figures/fig7_cap_ladder.pdf}
      \caption{Best rms versus leaf cap (top) with the winning tiling at
               each cap below. The dashed line fits caps 2--5; cap 6
               breaks the trend at $4.41\times10^{7}$, in the cap-5
               family. Caps 2 and 3 are $\tau$-only stacks; the
               $\lambda$ split appears at cap 4; the asymmetric deep
               stack at cap 5 drops the rms by more than a factor of
               two, and the sixth leaf (top, full $\lambda$ width) adds
               nothing. The jump is the allocation, not the leaf count.}
      \label{fig:caps}
   \end{figure}
   Two more paths were deleted. The columns-constrained optimizer never
   broke about $1.0\times10^{8}$, and the winning tiling (a root $\tau$
   cut with an asymmetric $\lambda$-column stack) is not expressible in
   the column family. Grid search reached only $1.64\times10^{8}$. Both
   were removed from the code along with the portfolio machinery.

\section{Stability across atmospheres}
\label{sec:cross}

   Everything so far ran on the solar-like G2 model. To test whether the
   ladder and the optima survive a different atmosphere, we repeated the
   cap ladder (caps 2 to 6, seeds 0 and 1, budget 800, staged path,
   window $[-5,\,3]$, smooth window 15) on the K dwarf model K4
   (485 rows, validated like G2) and then scored every winner on the
   other atmosphere with the same scorer.

   The K4 ladder finds its own asymmetric optimum, and a steeper one.
   Caps 2 and 3 stay $\tau$-only stacks at $1.088\times10^{8}$ and
   $9.60\times10^{7}$, cap 4 stays $\tau$-only at $7.85\times10^{7}$,
   and the $\lambda$ split appears at cap 5 ($3.46\times10^{7}$) and
   deepens at cap 6 ($2.93\times10^{7}$). The K4 cap-5 winner shares the
   family shape of the G2 optimum (a root $\tau$ cut with the depth
   stacked inside one $\lambda$ column) but puts the root cut at 1.07
   rather than 4.37 and the $\lambda$ cut at 3.94 rather than 3.83, and
   it stacks only two deep against three on G2. The cap-6 winner splits
   the sparse side as well, which G2 never rewards. Tiling shapes are in
   Fig.~\ref{fig:k4ladder}; winner trees are in trees/ with provenance
   in trees/README.md.

   \begin{figure}[t]
      \includegraphics[width=\hsize]{figures/fig8_k4_ladder.pdf}
      \caption{The cap ladder repeated on the K dwarf model K4 (top:
               best K4-native rms versus leaf cap, with the G2-native
               ladder for comparison; bottom: the winning K4 tiling at
               each cap). Caps 2 to 4 are $\tau$-only stacks; the
               $\lambda$ split appears at cap 5 and the sparse side
               splits at cap 6. The K4 optimum is steeper than G2: cap 6
               beats cap 5 by a further 15\%.}
      \label{fig:k4ladder}
   \end{figure}

   Neither atmosphere's optimum transfers. Scored foreign, the G2
   cap-5 optimum degrades from $4.32\times10^{7}$ to
   $1.35\times10^{8}$ on K4 (a factor of 3.1), and the K4 cap-5
   G2 (a factor of 4.6). The full matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:cross}) shows
   the same pattern at every cap: foreign losses at the cap winners run
   from 0.9 to 5.5, and no single tiling scores within a factor of two
   of the native optimum on both atmospheres at once. The one robust
   tilt is that the mid-tier tilings travel
   best: the G2 cap-4 winner scores $8.83\times10^{7}$ on K4, close
   to its $1.00\times10^{8}$ native value, and the plain seed tilings
   score near $9\times10^{7}$ on both atmospheres, while both
   atmospheres' sharp optima more than triple abroad. Sharpness does
   not travel; mediocrity does.

   \begin{figure}[t]
      \includegraphics[width=\hsize]{figures/fig9_cross_scores.pdf}
      \caption{Native versus foreign rms per leaf cap. Each group of
               four bars scores that cap's G2 winner and K4 winner on
               both atmospheres. Neither optimum transfers: the G2
               cap-5 optimum loses a factor of 3.1 on K4 and the K4
               cap-5 optimum loses a factor of 4.6 on G2. Mid-tier
               tilings (cap 4, plain seeds) stay near $10^{8}$ on both.}
      \label{fig:cross}
   \end{figure}

   The verdict is that the method transfers but the tables do not:
   the staged pipeline finds a comparable asymmetric optimum on K4,
   yet each atmosphere needs its own search, and a production table
   should either be optimized per target star or regularized toward
   the flatter tilings that score acceptably on both. The claim in
   Sect.~\ref{sec:discussion} that the relative ordering of binnings
   is robust to the atmosphere does not survive this test and is
   corrected there.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

   The dominant lever is \emph{where} the wavelength resolution is spent,
   not how much there is. The photospheric cooling peak is built in a
   narrow range of $\tau$ around unity; sub-bins that reach this regime
   carry most of the radiative energy exchange, and grouping across a wide
   wavelength range there smears the Planck mean. Splitting that one
   $\tau$-group in wavelength removes most of the error, while splitting
   the optically thin or thick groups adds bands but little accuracy
   (Fig.~\ref{fig:qrad}). The winning tiling of Sect.~\ref{sec:results}
   is this idea in its asymmetric form: depth resolution stacked in the
   dense column, the sparse side left whole. The beam search plus
   topology REALLOC found it from a frozen staged skeleton, which is the
   practical value of optimizing the partition directly rather than by
   edge-position proxy.

   Two caveats bound the present results. First, the transfer is 1D and
   each binning table is atmosphere specific: Sect.~\ref{sec:cross} shows
   neither the G2 nor the K4 optimum transfers (foreign losses of 3 to
   5), so absolute residuals must be recomputed per target star (and
   ideally extended to 3D mean opacities) for production tables.
   Second, each evaluation is a full transfer solve,
   so the search is a run-and-wait batch job; for a 3D-RHD context the
   optimized table would be produced once offline and read by the
   simulation, exactly as a hand-binned table is today.

   The deeper caveat is the basin-selection problem of
   Sect.~\ref{sec:negative}: the staged-$\lambda$ rms available at
   selection time does not predict the post-topology outcome, and nothing
   else cheap does either. Five directions remain open as future work:
   (a) late-selection portfolios that refine each member through topology
   before picking, correct but at about five times the budget; (b) wider
   transplant reach through multi-round chains, larger joint walks, or
   transplants across rather than inside columns; (c) basin diversity in
   the beam, keeping topologically distinct rather than just
   cost-distinct members so the deep-stack allocation survives grow;
   (d) objective shaping that rewards depth resolution where the sub-bin
   density lives, at the risk of biasing the physics; and (e) joint or
   regularized optimization over several atmospheres at once, since
   Sect.~\ref{sec:cross} shows sharp single-star optima do not transfer.

   The guillotine-tree representation is deliberately general: it
   subsumes the restricted layouts of earlier work as special cases and
   lets the data decide whether a $\tau$ or a $\lambda$ cut is warranted
   at each node. Nothing in the formulation ties it to the
   Planck/Rosseland blend of Eq.~\ref{eq:blend}; the objective
   (Eq.~\ref{eq:objective}) is the only thing that matters, and it could
   equally well target a different mean or a different transfer regime.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

   We have recast the opacity-binning partition as a guillotine-tree
   tiling of the $(-\log_{10}\tau,\log_{10}\lambda)$ plane and optimized
   it directly, by staged seeding plus non-greedy beam grow plus topology
   search, against the radiative heating-rate residual from a full
   transfer solve. On the solar-like G2 setup at a cap of 5 groups, the
   campaign over 25 seeds reaches $4.87\times10^{7}$ through a late
   REALLOC cliff, and verification re-runs reach $4.37\times10^{7}$,
   each far below the $1.318\times10^{8}$ seed tier. The winning tiling
   stacks $\tau$ depth in the dense $\lambda$ column (cuts at 1.02 and
   2.09 under a frozen root cut at 4.37 and a $\lambda$ cut at 3.83) and
   leaves the sparse side whole. The same pipeline on the K dwarf K4
   finds its own asymmetric optimum ($3.46\times10^{7}$ at cap 5,
   $2.93\times10^{7}$ at cap 6), but neither star's optimum transfers:
   foreign losses run 3 to 5, and only the flatter mid-tier tilings
   score near $10^{8}$ on both (Sect.~\ref{sec:cross}). Tables must be
   optimized per target star.
   What works is the combination: staged seeding finds candidate basins,
   beam grow refines them without early lock-in, and topology REALLOC
   escapes to the asymmetric allocation. The within-group opacity split
   deserves equal billing: it carries roughly four-fifths of the
   winner's accuracy, and its per-group dynamic ranges ($7.9\times10^{2}$
   to $1.3\times10^{8}$) explain why every group splits. What does not
   work is choosing between basins early: raw staged rms anti-predicts
   the refined outcome, portfolios with early selection lose the winning
   basin, column-constrained tilings cannot express it, and extra groups
   do not substitute for putting the depth where the density lives.
   Basin selection is the open problem, with late selection, wider
   transplants, beam diversity and objective shaping as the candidate
   answers. The split scheme was tested directly: fixed fractions score
   $2$--$5\times$ worse than the fitted breaks, a fourth segment never
   pays, and only wider pre-fit smoothing helps ($4.6\%$); it is now the
   default.

\section{Guillotine-tree representation and search details}
\label{app:search}

   A tree node is either a leaf, carrying the $(\tau,\lambda)$ window of
   its group, or an interior node carrying an axis ($\tau$ or $\lambda$)
   and a cut position together with two children. The bounding window is
   the union of all ODF steps in $(\log_{10}\lambda,\,-\log_{10}\tau)$.
   Leaves are enumerated in $\tau$-major depth-first order, which fixes
   the canonical band order
   $\mathrm{band}=\mathrm{group}\cdot N_\mathrm{splits}+\mathrm{segment}$.

   Staged seeding starts from 3 equally spaced $\tau$ cuts over the
   outer window. The Latin-hypercube scan samples 64 cut sets, scores
   each raw on a $\tau$-only tree, and keeps pairwise-separated winners
   for a light polish. The $\tau$-only polish is a coordinate-wise sweep
   to a fixed point. The flipped bottom-region scan then freezes the top
   seed cut and exhaustively enumerates wavelength-cut and per-column
   $\tau$-cut combinations over the photospheric region under the two
   asymmetric allocations (all spare cuts in the low-$\lambda$ column or
   all in the high-$\lambda$ column), keeping the best.

   In the grow stage, for each tree on the beam the 4 widest leaves are
   considered for splitting; for each, the split fractions 0.35, 0.50
   and 0.65 of the leaf extent are tried on each axis,
   the resulting child trees are scored with Eq.~\ref{eq:objective}, and
   the best 3 distinct trees (by leaf-rectangle signature) are kept for
   the next round. Growth stops at the leaf cap
   of 5 or when no trial split improves the
   residual beyond a relative threshold. The polish stage then performs a
   coordinate-wise sweep of every unfrozen cut position to a fixed point.
   Frozen staged cuts are never moved or removed by any later phase. The
   topology pass tries SPLIT, TRANSPLANT and REALLOC moves in turn, each
   polished to its position optimum and adopted only on strict
   improvement. The
   entire search is bounded by 800 evaluations or a
   wall-clock budget and can be interrupted, returning the best tree
   found.
